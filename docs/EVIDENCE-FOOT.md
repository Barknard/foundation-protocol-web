### Dead-end re-attempts (§1 of the task)

**Rathleff 2015 protocol/results — VERIFIED against primary full text.** Fetched the actual PDF (fysioechografie.nl mirror renders as readable text via the Read tool, unlike WebFetch which chokes on the PDF byte stream). Confirms word-for-word: 3s concentric / 2s isometric hold / 3s eccentric tempo; weeks 1-2 = 3×12RM bodyweight; "after 2 weeks" (i.e., wk3-4) = 4×10RM with backpack+books added load; "after 4 weeks" (i.e., wk5+) = 5×8RM; every second day; 3-month block. Table in EVIDENCE-FOOT.md §2f is accurate as written. Primary endpoint CONFIRMED: 3-month FFI 29 points lower in strength group (95% CI 6-52, P=0.016).

**CORRECTION to §2b (and to the synthesis claim "strength > stretch"):** the primary source adds a material qualifier that the original evidence pass did not capture — "At 1, 6, and 12 months, there were no differences between groups (P > 0.34). At 12 months, the FFI was 22 points (95% CI 9-36) in the strength group and 16 points (95% CI 0-32) in the stretch group." The strength group's advantage is real and clinically meaningful but is a **3-month, not a 12-month, effect** — both arms converge to statistically indistinguishable outcomes by 6 and 12 months. The paper's own discussion states this plainly: "High-load strength training was not associated with better long-term outcome at 12 months, but only with a better short-term effect after 3 months." Separately, the specific claim in §2b that the strength group had "fewer follow-up/rescue treatments" is **not supported** by anything in the primary text (the paper reports satisfaction ratings, not rescue-treatment counts, and the 12-month satisfaction difference is not statistically significant: RR 3.1, 95% CI 0.6-15.0) — this looks like an unverified embellishment from the original pass and is corrected in §2b below.

**DiGiovanni 2003/2006 (JBJS) — VERIFIED against primary full text (2006 2-year follow-up paper).** Dosage CONFIRMED exactly: seated, cross leg, pull toes toward shin, hold 10s × 10 reps × 3/day. Enrollment/completion numbers CONFIRMED: 101 enrolled in the 2003 phase-one RCT, 82 completed the 8-week evaluation (46 stretch-group/Group A, 36 Achilles-group/Group B); 82 were mailed the 2-year follow-up survey, 66 (39 A / 27 B, 80% response) returned complete data.

**CORRECTION to §2c — this is the most substantive correction in this pass.** EVIDENCE-FOOT.md's original text says "at 2-year follow-up, the plantar-fascia-stretch group reported significantly less pain and higher satisfaction... fewer going on to more invasive treatment" as if Group A's advantage over Group B persisted to 2 years. **This is not what the trial found and the framing is backwards.** The 2003 8-week trial was NOT a pure parallel-group design carried to 2 years — it was a crossover: at the 8-week mark, **all Group B (Achilles-stretch) patients were switched onto the plantar-fascia-specific stretch protocol** ("At the conclusion of phase one, all patients managed with the Achilles tendon-stretching protocol were given the plantar fascia-specific stretching protocol"). So by the 2-year mark, both groups had been doing the SAME (PF-specific) stretch for nearly two years, and the 2006 paper explicitly reports **no significant difference between groups at 2 years** (worst pain p=0.78, first-step morning pain p=0.997) — the opposite of "the PF-stretch group reported significantly less pain." The encouraging 2-year descriptive numbers (92% satisfied, 94% reporting less/no pain, 77% no activity limitation, only 18% needing added treatment, none went to surgery) describe the **combined cohort under the PF-stretch protocol**, not a maintained between-arm difference. The genuine, correctly-attributable finding is: PF-specific stretch beat Achilles-only stretch at 8 weeks (pain-at-worst p=0.03, first-step morning pain p=0.009); once everyone got the effective stretch, outcomes converged and stayed good at 2 years. Corrected inline in §2c below. This doesn't change what the app prescribes (PF-specific stretch is still the right call), but it changes the *causal claim* the app's citation copy should make.

**Bonus finding from primary text, useful for §6a:** DiGiovanni's own discussion recommends "the plantar fascia-specific stretching protocol for six months and, if it is not successful at that point, then another treatment approach should be pursued" — a concrete, source-backed 6-month decision point that aligns with (and slightly firms up) the existing "3-6 months typical" language in §6a.

**Riel 2019 self-dosed vs pre-determined HSR trial — VERIFIED against primary full text (Journal of Physiotherapy 65:144-151), and this is a bigger freshness finding than the original pass captured.** n=70 (35/35), both groups did the SAME Rathleff-style heavy-slow-resistance heel raise for 12 weeks, differing only in self-dosed (as heavy as tolerable, ≤8RM) vs pre-determined (fixed 12RM→10RM→8RM) loading. Primary outcome (Foot Health Status Questionnaire pain domain) showed **no significant between-group difference** (adjusted MD -7 points, 95% CI -16 to 2) — CONFIRMED, self-dosed and clinician-set progression are equivalent, as the original pass said.

**CORRECTION/ADDITION — the original pass omitted the trial's headline finding.** Only **4 of 70 participants total (3/35 self-dosed, 1/35 pre-determined) reached "Patient Acceptable Symptom State"** at 12 weeks — roughly 6%, in a cohort doing a protocol structurally identical to Rathleff's. Both groups still improved by more than the minimum clinically important difference on pain, and Global Rating of Change showed most participants "improved" (24/33 and 20/32) — so the exercise is doing something real — but full/satisfactory resolution within a single 12-week HSR block is the exception, not the norm, even under ideal RCT-supervised conditions. The Riel paper's own discussion cites a 2019 network meta-analysis (Babatunde et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2019;53:182-194) comparing common plantar-heel-pain treatments and finding **none was superior to any other** — direct evidence that the field's confidence in HSR's superiority has already been tempered by 2019, before the current 2023-2026 freshness window even starts. This materially softens any in-app language that implies "do the heel raise for 12 weeks and expect to be done."

### Freshness sweep 2023-2026 (§2 of the task)

**Finding 1 — the biggest one: high-load strength training's "best-evidenced exercise" status does not hold up under a wider read, even though the single Rathleff RCT itself is sound.** Three independent lines of evidence converge:
- A 2024 systematic review with meta-analysis of therapeutic exercise for plantar fasciitis (Ferreira et al./MLTJ 14(1), 2024, PROSPERO CRD42021296710) found **"All 10 studies used [to test] stretch exercises, but only one used strengthening exercises"** — i.e., across the whole 2024-searched therapeutic-exercise literature, Rathleff 2015 is still the only strengthening RCT; stretching has ten times the trial base.
- The **2021 BJSM international best-practice guide** for plantar heel pain (Morrissey et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2021;55:1106-1118, PMC8458083) — co-authored by **Rathleff himself** — put plantar-fascia stretching + low-Dye taping + education in the mandatory "DO" core tier, and explicitly **excluded strengthening from the hierarchy**, stating "our stringent systematic review could not identify evidence in favour of more comprehensive exercise approaches" and that the expert panel (Rathleff included) was "divided on whether such resistance exercises are useful or not." That the protocol's own originator co-signed a guideline that declined to elevate it is a strong, direct signal.
- Rathleff's own follow-up trial (Riel 2019, verified above) found only ~6% of patients doing the HSR protocol reach a satisfactory symptom state within 12 weeks, and the trial's discussion cites **Babatunde et al.'s 2019 network meta-analysis** (*Br J Sports Med* 53:182-194) which found no plantar-heel-pain treatment was superior to any other.

None of this means the heel raise is bad advice — the RCT data (29-point FFI advantage at 3 months, P=0.016) is real, and the app's use of it is reasonable and low-risk. But EVIDENCE-FOOT.md's original framing ("strong single RCT + replication," "the single best-evidenced exercise in the whole feature") overstates its evidentiary status relative to the PF-specific stretch, which has the stronger multi-study, multi-guideline consensus behind it. Corrected in §2b/§7 below.

## 1. Foot-pain sub-locations a lay person can self-identify

**Grade: expert consensus / clinical-reference synthesis (this is descriptive/triage content, not an RCT-tested intervention — graded accordingly).**

A tappable foot map with 5 zones is clinically sensible because these zones map onto genuinely distinct injury mechanisms and, largely, distinct management pathways. Sources are clinical references (Cleveland Clinic, Mayo Clinic, podiatric/orthopedic summaries) cross-checked against the condition-specific literature used elsewhere in this doc rather than a single RCT — this section is triage logic, not a treatment protocol, so "evidence grade" here means "matches consistent clinical consensus," not "RCT-proven."

| Zone | Most likely culprits | App can self-manage | Needs a clinician |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Heel (bottom)** | Plantar fasciitis (plantar heel pain) — by far the most common; fat-pad atrophy/bruise (older adults, sudden onset after a hard landing); calcaneal stress fracture (uncommon, but the key differential — see red flags) | Plantar fasciitis: yes, this is the flagship protocol (Section 2) | Suspected calcaneal stress fracture (focal bone tenderness, pain at rest/night, recent load spike); fat-pad pain that doesn't respond to standard PF care in a few weeks (different tissue, different management) |
| **Arch** | Plantar fasciitis extending into the arch (the fascia runs heel-to-forefoot, so arch pain is often just PF with a different pain center); posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD) — pain more on the inside of the ankle/arch, often with a sense of the arch flattening, more common in those with existing flat feet or an inciting ankle inversion/overuse event | Arch pain consistent with PF: yes, same protocol as heel | PTTD is a different tendon problem that can progress to a structural flatfoot deformity if missed — arch pain with visible/felt arch collapse, pain more medial-ankle than mid-sole, or swelling along the inside of the ankle should route to a clinician rather than the PF protocol |
| **Ball of foot** | Metatarsalgia (general forefoot overload); Morton's neuroma (nerve-specific, classically 3rd/4th toe web space); capsulitis of a lesser MTP joint; sesamoiditis (under the big toe specifically); rarely, metatarsal stress fracture | Metatarsalgia without neural signs: yes (Section 3) | Any burning/numbness/tingling between toes or "pebble in sock" feeling (possible Morton's neuroma); focal bone tenderness or pain at rest (possible stress fracture); pain isolated under the big toe with a MTP joint mechanism (possible sesamoiditis, a distinct condition) |
| **Toes** | Bunion (hallux valgus, gradual, medial big-toe-joint bump); hallux rigidus (gradual stiffness, loss of big-toe upward bend); turf toe (sudden hyperextension injury, usually sports-linked); gout (sudden, severe, red/hot/swollen, classically overnight) | None of these are well-served by a generic "foot exercise" prescription — toe conditions are structural (bunion/hallux rigidus) or acute-inflammatory (gout) rather than load-management problems | All of the above genuinely benefit from clinician evaluation: bunions/hallux rigidus for footwear/orthotic/possible surgical planning, gout because it's a systemic metabolic condition requiring medication, turf toe because forced return before healing risks chronic joint damage. **This app should route ALL toe-zone taps to "see a clinician" rather than attempting a self-management protocol** — it's the one zone where the evidence doesn't support a simple exercise-based fix |
| **Top of foot** | Extensor tendinitis (aching along the tendons from ankle to toes, often footwear-pressure or overuse related, ~20-30% of dorsal foot pain); metatarsal or (less common but higher-stakes) navicular stress fracture; bone spurs | Mild extensor tendinitis (footwear/lacing adjustment + relative rest) — thin evidence base, not a headline feature | Any pinpoint bone tenderness, swelling localized over one spot rather than diffuse along a tendon line, pain at rest/night, or recent training-load spike — these point toward stress fracture, especially navicular (a high-risk, easily-missed site — see Section 5) |

**What the app should do:** Build the foot map as the entry point, but only author full exercise protocols for the two zones with strong-to-moderate exercise evidence (heel/plantar-fascia-adjacent arch pain -> Section 2 protocol; ball-of-foot without neural signs -> Section 3 protocol). Every other zone tap (toes, top-of-foot, arch-with-medial-ankle-features, or ANY zone with red-flag features from Section 5) should present a "this needs a clinician, here's why" message rather than a home exercise program — this keeps the feature honest about where the evidence actually supports self-management vs. where it doesn't.

## 2. Plantar heel pain (plantar fasciitis)

**Grade: strong (CPG-backed + multiple RCTs) for the core protocol; grade A/B per JOSPT's own scale on the specific pieces below.**

### 2a. What the 2023 JOSPT CPG actually says

The Academy of Orthopaedic Physical Therapy / American Academy of Sports Physical Therapy revised the Heel Pain–Plantar Fasciitis CPG in 2023 (Koc et al., *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 2023;53(12):CPG1-CPG39, doi:10.2519/jospt.2023.0303 — a revision of the 2014 CPG, incorporating 100+ new studies). JOSPT's own grading scale: **A = strong evidence, B = moderate evidence, C = weak evidence, D = conflicting evidence, F = expert opinion.** Recommendations (compiled from CPG summaries — the full-text PDF was paywalled/403 for direct fetch, so grade letters below are as reported by secondary summaries and should be treated as approximate pending direct CPG access):

- **Manual therapy** (ankle/foot joint + soft-tissue mobilization) — recommended to reduce pain and improve function, generally paired with stretching/exercise rather than standalone.
- **Stretching** — standing gastroc-soleus (calf) stretch AND seated plantar-fascia-specific stretch — recommended, among the strongest-supported interventions.
- **Foot orthoses** (prefabricated, not necessarily custom) — recommended for short-to-medium term pain relief.
- **Taping** (e.g. low-Dye) — recommended for short-term relief/arch support, adjunct only.
- **Night splints** — recommended specifically for symptoms persisting beyond ~6 months, not as first-line.
- **Strengthening** (foot intrinsic muscles + proximal hip/lower-limb) — recommended, though the CPG's own framing leans toward general/intrinsic strengthening rather than singling out the Rathleff high-load heel-raise protocol as its own graded line item.
- **Ultrasound, laser, dry needling** — limited evidence; should not replace active exercise-based care.
- **Patient education** (activity modification, footwear, self-management) — foundational, not optional.

This app should treat the CPG as the umbrella (education + calf/PF stretching + orthotic/footwear advice + activity modification), and layer the higher-power RCT protocol below (Rathleff high-load strengthening) on top as the "strength" arm, since it is the best single head-to-head RCT and postdates/extends the CPG's general "strengthening" line.

### 2b. High-load strength training (Rathleff protocol) — grade: solid single RCT, NOT independently replicated, NOT guideline-endorsed at the same tier as stretching [CORRECTED 2026-08-17 — see Verification pass Finding 1]

**Study:** Rathleff MS, Mølgaard CM, Fredberg U, et al. "High-load strength training improves outcome in patients with plantar fasciitis: A randomized controlled trial with 12-month follow-up." *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* 2015;25(3):e292-e300 (originally presented 2014, published online ahead of print — often cited as "Rathleff 2014/2015"). 48 patients with ultrasound-verified plantar fasciitis, randomized to shoe inserts + plantar-fascia stretching vs. shoe inserts + high-load strength training, exercising every second day.

**Exact protocol (this is the concrete prescription to build into the app):**
- Exercise: **unilateral (single-leg) heel raise standing on a step edge, with a rolled towel inserted under the toes** so the toes are held maximally dorsiflexed — this pre-tensions the plantar fascia via the windlass mechanism so the heel raise loads the fascia itself, not just the calf.
- Tempo: **3-second concentric (rise) + 2-second isometric hold at the top + 3-second eccentric (lower)** — a slow, controlled "heavy-slow resistance" tempo.
- Dosage/progression over 12 weeks:
  - Weeks 1-2: 3 sets x 12RM (bodyweight only), every other day (~3-4x/week).
  - Weeks 3-4: add external load via a weighted backpack (books); 4 sets x 10RM.
  - Weeks 5-12: 5 sets x 8RM, continuing to add load in the backpack as tolerated (progressive overload) — i.e., reps go DOWN and sets/load go UP as the person gets stronger, classic heavy-slow-resistance progression.
- Duration: 12 weeks minimum, performed every second day (not daily — this is a heavy strength stimulus that needs recovery between sessions).

**Results:** At the primary 3-month endpoint, the strength group's Foot Function Index (FFI) was 29 points lower (better) than the stretch group's [95% CI 6-52, P=0.016] — a large, clinically meaningful difference, VERIFIED word-for-word against the primary paper. **[CORRECTED 2026-08-17]** However, the primary paper itself reports: "At 1, 6, and 12 months, there were no differences between groups (P > 0.34)." The strength group's advantage is a **3-month effect, not a durable 12-month one** — by 12 months FFI was 22 (strength) vs 16 (stretch), not significantly different. The paper's own discussion states plainly: "High-load strength training was not associated with better long-term outcome at 12 months, but only with a better short-term effect after 3 months." The original claim that the strength group had "fewer follow-up/rescue treatments" was **not found in the primary source** and is removed — the primary source's actual secondary finding is a non-significant trend toward higher *treatment satisfaction* at 3 and 12 months (RR 2.3 and 3.1, both CIs crossing 1). A follow-up trial (Riel et al. 2019/Rathleff group, self-dosed vs. predetermined progressive heavy-slow-resistance, VERIFIED against primary text) found self-dosed and clinician-set progression produced similar results — supporting flexibility in exactly how load is added, which is good news for an app that can't supervise in person — **but also found only 4 of 70 participants (~6%) reached "Patient Acceptable Symptom State" within the 12-week block**, and its discussion cites a 2019 network meta-analysis (Babatunde et al., *Br J Sports Med* 53:182-194) finding no plantar-heel-pain treatment superior to any other. See "Verification pass" section at the top of this file (Finding 1) for the fuller freshness-sweep picture: a 2024 systematic review found only 1 of 10 exercise-therapy RCTs in the field tested strengthening (the rest tested stretching), and the 2021 BJSM international best-practice guide — co-authored by Rathleff himself — declined to include strengthening in its core recommended tier, citing insufficient evidence and a divided expert panel.

**What the app should do:** Prescribe this as the primary strength exercise for plantar heel pain — single-leg heel raise, towel under toes, 3-2-3 tempo, every other day, 12-week minimum block, starting bodyweight/12RM and progressing toward heavier/lower-rep as tolerated (app can simplify "backpack + books" progression to "add a loaded backpack" or step up in a simple weighted-vest/dumbbell-in-hand equivalent). **[SOFTENED 2026-08-17]** This is a well-evidenced, low-risk, fast-acting exercise (real 3-month benefit) — but it should be framed in-app as *complementary to*, not more proven than, the plantar-fascia-specific stretch below, which has broader guideline and multi-study support. Do not present the heel raise as "the single best-evidenced exercise in the feature" (the original framing here and in §7) — present both as core, evidence-backed, and mutually reinforcing.

### 2c. Plantar-fascia-specific stretch (DiGiovanni protocol) — grade: strong RCT, 2-year follow-up

**Studies:** DiGiovanni BF et al. "Tissue-specific plantar fascia-stretching exercise enhances outcomes in patients with chronic heel pain: a prospective, randomized study." *J Bone Joint Surg Am* 2003;85(7):1270-1277 (101 patients, chronic PF >=10 months, randomized to plantar-fascia-specific stretch vs. Achilles/calf-stretch-only, evaluated at 8 weeks). DiGiovanni BF et al. "Plantar fascia-specific stretching exercise improves outcomes in patients with chronic plantar fasciitis: a prospective clinical trial with two-year follow-up." *J Bone Joint Surg Am* 2006;88(8):1775-1781 (82 of the original patients followed 2 years).

**Exact protocol:**
- Sit down, cross the affected leg over the opposite knee.
- Grip the base of the toes (across the toes, near the ball of the foot) with the fingers of the same-side hand.
- Pull the toes back toward the shin until a stretch is felt along the arch/plantar fascia (can palpate the fascia band with the other hand to confirm tension).
- **Hold 10 seconds, repeat 10 times, 3x per day** (before first steps in the morning, before standing after sitting, and before/after activity).

**Results — VERIFIED against primary text, and CORRECTED 2026-08-17:** The plantar-fascia-specific stretch group (Group A) had significantly better pain and function outcomes at 8 weeks than the Achilles-stretch-only group (Group B): pain-at-worst p=0.03, first-step morning pain p=0.009. **The original claim here — that "the improvement was durable... at 2-year follow-up, the plantar-fascia-stretch group reported significantly less pain and higher satisfaction" — is backwards and is corrected.** This was NOT a parallel-group comparison carried to 2 years: at the 8-week mark, **all Group B patients were switched onto the plantar-fascia-specific stretch** ("all patients managed with the Achilles tendon-stretching protocol were given the plantar fascia-specific stretching protocol"). By 2 years, both groups had been doing the same effective stretch for nearly two years, and the 2006 paper explicitly reports **no significant difference between groups at 2 years** (worst pain p=0.78, first-step morning pain p=0.997) — the two arms converged, they did not stay apart. The encouraging 2-year numbers (92% satisfied, 94% less/no pain, 77% no activity limitation, only 18% needed added treatment, none went to surgery) describe the **combined 66-patient cohort under the PF-stretch protocol**, not a maintained advantage of one arm over the other. The correctly-attributable finding is: PF-specific stretch beat Achilles-only stretch at 8 weeks; once everyone got the effective stretch, outcomes converged and stayed good at 2 years. DiGiovanni's own discussion recommends using the protocol for six months, and switching approach if it hasn't worked by then — a concrete, source-backed decision point worth citing in §6a. Note: Rathleff's later high-load strengthening RCT showed a larger benefit at 3 months specifically (see 2b) but that advantage does not persist to 6/12 months either — so the evidence does NOT cleanly support a "strength > stretch" hierarchy the way the original text here claimed; see Verification pass Finding 1. Both are evidence-backed; the stretch has the broader/longer evidence base (single-RCT-with-2-year-follow-up plus multiple corroborating stretching trials in the 2024 systematic review), the heel raise has the larger short-term effect size in its one RCT.

**What the app should do:** Prescribe this stretch specifically for morning first-step pain — cue it as "do this before your feet hit the floor" — as a companion to, not a replacement for, the heel-raise strength work.

### 2d. Calf / gastroc-soleus stretching — grade: moderate, mechanistic rationale, mixed head-to-head

Tight gastrocnemius-soleus is theorized to increase pronation and load transfer to the plantar fascia at push-off (reduced ankle dorsiflexion range is a documented risk factor). Evidence that standing gastroc-soleus stretching alone reduces PF symptoms is positive but weaker/more mixed than the plantar-fascia-specific stretch or high-load strengthening: one RCT (Barry et al. 2002, comparing standing calf stretch to a night splint) found the night-splint group recovered faster with fewer follow-ups than the stretching group — i.e., calf stretch alone is not the strongest lever, but it's still endorsed in the JOSPT CPG (paired with PF-specific stretch) and helps address a plausible contributing biomechanical factor (ankle dorsiflexion deficit — this is the SAME dorsiflexion-deficit link relevant to metatarsalgia below). Cite: Porter D et al. *J Sport Rehabil* 2013 (gastroc-soleus stretch RCT); Barry LD et al. 2002 (night splint vs. stretch comparison, *J Foot Ankle Surg*).

**What the app should do:** Include a standing calf stretch (straight-knee for gastrocnemius, bent-knee for soleus, ~30s x 3 each, daily) as a secondary/supporting piece, not the headline exercise — it likely already exists in the app's general mobility library and can simply be tagged as relevant to foot pain rather than authored fresh.

### 2e. Night splints, orthoses, taping — grade: moderate, adjunct only

Night splints (holding the ankle/toes dorsiflexed overnight to prevent the fascia shortening while asleep, which is the mechanistic reason for first-step pain) are CPG-recommended specifically for symptoms persisting beyond ~6 months, not as a default first-line tool — and evidence is mixed on comfort/adherence (splints can disrupt sleep). Prefabricated foot orthoses have short-to-medium-term evidence for pain relief. These are adjuncts an app can *mention* (e.g. "if pain persists past 2-3 months, a night splint or off-the-shelf arch-support insole may help") but should not be the core prescribed "exercise" since they aren't behaviors the app programs/progresses the way it does strength and stretch work.

## 3. Ball-of-foot pain (metatarsalgia)

**Grade: thin — say so honestly.** Metatarsalgia has nowhere near the RCT base plantar fasciitis has. A dedicated review states plainly that there is little level-I evidence supporting the efficacy of conservative treatment for metatarsalgia, despite conservative care being universally recommended as first-line (orthojournal.org / ScienceDirect metatarsalgia reviews). Everything below should be flagged in-app (or at least in dev docs) as lower-confidence than the plantar fasciitis protocol.

### 3a. Intrinsic foot muscle strengthening (short-foot exercise, toe curls) — grade: thin, mechanistic + tiny studies

**Short-foot exercise:** contracting the intrinsic foot muscles to shorten the foot and lift the arch without curling the toes, done seated or standing. Evidence base is a mix of small RCTs/pilot studies showing the intrinsic muscles do activate during SFE and toe-curl-type exercises (EMG/MRI studies e.g. Kwon 2016 T2-weighted MRI activation study — full citation not independently re-verified here) and evidence that SFE improves postural balance, navicular drop, and excess pronation. **The most specific study found for metatarsalgia+Morton's-toe** is Yoo WG, *J Phys Ther Sci* 2014;26(12):1997-1998 — but this is a **single-subject case report** (n=1, 2 weeks, 50 reps/day of a combined forefoot-adduction + interphalangeal-flexion / short-foot + MTP-flexion exercise), showing improved pressure pain threshold (1.0->1.5 kg), reduced peak contact pressure (0.63->0.50 kg/cm²), and improved navicular drop. This is genuinely weak evidence (case-report level, not RCT) — it demonstrates plausibility and a safe mechanism, not proven efficacy. A systematic review of intrinsic foot strengthening in asymptomatic athletes (ScienceDirect 2024) found no meta-analysis exists establishing its importance even in healthy populations, let alone metatarsalgia patients specifically.

**Toe curls (towel curls):** engage flexor digitorum longus/brevis, lumbricals, flexor hallucis longus. Used clinically alongside short-foot exercise as standard conservative care for plantar conditions generally; evidence is largely extrapolated from the plantar-fasciitis literature and general foot-core research rather than metatarsalgia-specific trials.

**What the app should do:** Include short-foot exercise and/or toe curls as a plausible, low-risk, low-cost component (they cost nothing to try and have a sound biomechanical rationale — strengthening the arch-supporting intrinsics theoretically redistributes forefoot load), but the in-app copy should NOT claim strong evidence — frame as "commonly recommended, evidence is still thin" rather than citing it with the confidence of the PF heel-raise.

### 3b. Calf stretching / ankle dorsiflexion — grade: moderate mechanistic link, stronger than the strengthening evidence

This is actually one of the better-evidenced pieces for metatarsalgia. DiGiovanni CW, Sangeorzan BJ, Hansen ST ("Isolated gastrocnemius tightness," *J Bone Joint Surg* 2002) found isolated gastrocnemius-soleus contracture (limited ankle dorsiflexion with the knee extended) was substantially more prevalent in patients with forefoot/midfoot pain than in controls — reported dorsiflexion averaged ~4.5 degrees in the patient group vs. ~13.1 degrees in controls with the knee extended (different secondary sources cite contracture-prevalence figures in roughly the 65-88% patient vs. 24-44% control range depending on the cutoff used — treat the precise percentages as approximate pending direct primary-source verification, but the direction and magnitude of the association is consistent across summaries). Mechanistically: a tight gastroc-soleus limits ankle dorsiflexion, which increases submetatarsal-head pressure during push-off, directly overloading the forefoot — this is the SAME calf-tightness mechanism implicated in plantar fasciitis (Section 2d), so a calf stretch is one of the few exercises with a legitimate evidence-based claim to help BOTH conditions.

**What the app should do:** Prescribe the same standing gastrocnemius + soleus stretch used for plantar fasciitis (Section 2d) for metatarsalgia too — this is genuine, efficient overlap, not padding. This is a "may already exist in the app" item per the task brief.

### 3c. Forefoot offloading / footwear / relative rest — grade: moderate (biomechanical evidence strong, clinical outcome RCTs thin)

- **Metatarsal pads**, placed just PROXIMAL to (behind) the metatarsal heads (not underneath them — that's a common misuse that doesn't offload anything), measurably shift plantar pressure from the metatarsal heads to the metatarsal shafts in pressure-mapping studies (Lower Extremity Review summaries of metatarsal pad biomechanics literature).
- **Rocker-sole shoes** reduce peak forefoot pressure during push-off by roughly 30-40% in pressure studies and are a documented, favorable intervention for forefoot load-distribution disorders including metatarsalgia.
- **Relative rest**: limiting high-impact activity (running, jumping) for an initial 4-6 week window, with daily step-count/impact moderation, is standard first-line advice in clinical treatment summaries (e.g. Medscape metatarsalgia treatment protocol), NOT complete immobilization — consistent with the general load-management principle in Section 4.
- Evidence quality note: pressure/biomechanics studies here are solid, but studies connecting "pressure changed" to "patient-reported pain resolved, RCT-confirmed" specifically for metatarsalgia are sparse — this is the same "thin" caveat as the rest of Section 3.

**What the app should do:** The app is a training program, not a footwear-fitting service, so the concrete, buildable piece is the RELATIVE REST / activity-modification message (reduce impact activity, don't stop moving) plus a simple footwear nudge ("look for a shoe with a rockered or stiff-but-cushioned forefoot, and avoid thin/flat/hard-soled shoes while this settles") rather than trying to prescribe orthotic pads the app can't fit.

### 3d. Distinguishing Morton's neuroma — grade: consistent clinical consensus, not a treatable-by-exercise condition

Morton's neuroma is a perineural fibrosis of an interdigital nerve (classically the 3rd/4th web space, occasionally 2nd/3rd) — mechanically and pathologically distinct from metatarsalgia's bone/joint overload, and it does NOT respond to the same strengthening/offloading protocol as metatarsalgia. Distinguishing features (consistent across Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic, and podiatric clinical summaries):

- **Location:** neuroma pain is between two specific toes (web space); metatarsalgia pain is under the metatarsal heads (across the ball of the foot generally).
- **Character:** neuroma produces burning, electric/shooting pain, numbness, or tingling radiating into the toes, and the classic "pebble in my sock" or "bunched-up sock" sensation that persists even with the shoe off; metatarsalgia is an ache/pressure pain, without numbness or tingling, that's worse with weight-bearing and better with rest/offloading.
- **Mulder's click:** squeezing the metatarsal heads together while pressing the interdigital space produces a palpable click AND reproduces the patient's symptoms — this is the most sensitive clinical sign for neuroma, but pain alone WITHOUT the click is not diagnostic and may just be metatarsalgia, bursitis, or a stress injury.

**What the app should do:** At the ball-of-foot branch of the foot-map flow, ask a simple differentiating question before prescribing anything: "Is there any burning, numbness, tingling, or a feeling like there's a pebble or bunched sock between two of your toes?" A yes routes to "see a clinician — this pattern needs a professional exam, not a home exercise program" (neuromas can be managed conservatively by a clinician — padding, footwear, sometimes injection — but self-diagnosis-and-treat is not appropriate here). A no (ache/pressure under the ball of the foot, no neural symptoms) routes to the metatarsalgia protocol (3a-3c).

### 3e. Gradual return — grade: consistent with general load-management principle (Section 4), metatarsalgia-specific RCTs on return timelines are essentially absent

Clinical summaries converge on: relative rest/reduced impact for the acute window (days to ~4-6 weeks depending on severity), then a gradual, criteria-based (not purely calendar-based) return to impact activity, mirroring the bone-stress-injury "optimal loading" principle even though metatarsalgia itself usually isn't a bone injury (see Section 5 for when it might be). No metatarsalgia-specific graded-return RCT was found in this search — this recommendation is extrapolated from the same load-management literature used in Section 4 and from general clinical practice summaries, not a metatarsalgia-specific trial. Flag as thin/extrapolated.

## 4. Load management during foot rehab

**Grade: strong (RCT-backed pain-monitoring model; CPG-consistent activity-modification consensus).**

### 4a. The pain-monitoring model (Silbernagel)

**Study:** Silbernagel KG, Thomeé R, Eriksson BI, Karlsson J. "Continued Sports Activity, Using a Pain-Monitoring Model, during Rehabilitation in Patients with Achilles Tendinopathy: A Randomized Controlled Study." *American Journal of Sports Medicine* 2007;35(6):897-906. 38 patients with Achilles tendinopathy randomized to (a) continue tendon-loading activity (running, jumping) using pain as a guide, vs. (b) stop all such loading activity for the first 6 weeks ("active rest"). Both groups did the same eccentric-biased exercise program otherwise.

**The rule, exactly as tested:** pain during and after activity is allowed to reach **up to 5 out of 10** on a numerical pain rating scale, PROVIDED it settles back to baseline by the next morning and doesn't progressively worsen session to session. Continuing to load under this rule produced no worse outcomes than stopping activity — in fact the continued-activity group did at least as well, with no evidence of harm from continuing to train through monitored pain.

This is Achilles-tendon research, not foot-specific, but it is THE evidence base cited across the sports-medicine literature (and already used elsewhere in Foundation Protocol's own injury protocol, per docs/EVIDENCE-REVIEW.md Section 4/Injury protocol) for how much pain is "acceptable" during rehab of a load-related musculoskeletal condition — and it generalizes cleanly to plantar fasciitis (a fascia/enthesis condition managed the same load-tolerant way as tendinopathy) and, with somewhat less direct evidence, to metatarsalgia (which is a joint/bone overload condition rather than fascia/tendon, but the same "monitor, don't avoid" principle is standard clinical practice per the metatarsalgia summaries in Section 3).

**Foot-specific pain thresholds found in the return-to-running literature:** during a run/loading session, pain staying at or below ~3/10 is considered an acceptable load; if next-morning heel pain is the same or better than baseline, that session was within a manageable range — this is a slightly more conservative foot-specific adaptation of Silbernagel's general 0-5/10 tendon rule, likely because plantar fascia and forefoot structures are directly weight-bearing on every step (harder to "rest between reps" the way a gym exercise allows) — app should use the more conservative foot-specific ~3/10-during, back-to-baseline-by-morning rule rather than the looser tendon-only 5/10 rule, to be safe.

### 4b. Relative rest / activity modification beats complete rest

Across both conditions, the literature converges on the same message found in the main program's own injury-protocol logic (PEACE & LOVE > RICE, per docs/EVIDENCE-REVIEW.md): complete rest is not the answer. For plantar fasciitis, prolonged rest does not improve long-term outcomes because recovery depends on rebuilding tissue capacity (mechanotransduction/collagen synthesis) under load, not on eliminating load; short periods of reduced activity help, but connective tissue that never gets reloaded doesn't adapt. For metatarsalgia, clinical protocols call for "relative rest" (limit running/jumping/high-impact for 4-6 weeks, moderate but don't eliminate daily steps) rather than complete offloading. This directly matches Foundation Protocol's existing PEACE & LOVE framing (Load, Optimism, Vascularization, Exercise) — foot pain should slot into the SAME "overuse/gradual-onset -> progressive loading, not rest" bucket already defined for tendinopathy in EVIDENCE-REVIEW.md's injury protocol, rather than requiring a new, separate injury-handling philosophy.

### 4c. Strength work continues, impact/running is what gets modified

A consistent pattern across the PF and metatarsalgia literature: the STRENGTH/exercise component of rehab (heel raises, calf stretch, intrinsic foot work) is meant to continue and progress even while symptomatic (governed by the pain-monitoring rule), while it's specifically RUNNING and other high-impact activity that gets throttled back or paused. This maps directly onto Foundation Protocol's existing architecture: general strength training (upper body, non-foot-loading lower body work like seated/supported exercises) can likely continue close to normal; foot-loading strength work (the prescribed heel raise / short-foot work) continues but IS the treatment, governed by the pain rule; running/impact cardio is the piece that pauses or drops to a lower-impact substitute (walking, cycling, swimming, elliptical — all mentioned in the return-to-running literature as acceptable impact-free conditioning during PF/metatarsalgia rehab) until the return-to-impact criteria below are met.

### 4d. Return-to-impact criteria

From the return-to-running-after-PF literature (multiple clinical/PT sources converging, not a single RCT — grade this as consistent clinical consensus rather than a controlled trial finding):
- **Pain-free walking** across normal daily distances/duration is the gate before any running is reintroduced.
- A commonly cited strength marker: **20 single-leg heel raises without reproducing symptoms** as a readiness check before loading up to running (this pairs naturally with the Rathleff heel-raise protocol already being trained — the same exercise IS the return-to-run test).
- Return to running should be gradual/graded (run-walk style reintroduction, small volume increases) rather than resuming at pre-injury volume — this is the same graded philosophy Foundation Protocol already uses for its main running progression (+10% session cap, per EVIDENCE-REVIEW.md) and should reuse that existing mechanism rather than invent a new one.
- During reintroduction, apply the pain-monitoring rule from 4a session-by-session: pain <=3/10 during, back to baseline by next morning = proceed; pain climbing or morning symptoms worse = hold/regress a step.

## 5. Red flags for foot pain

**Grade: expert consensus / clinical-safety-screening literature (this is safety scaffolding, evaluated the same way the existing injury-protocol red-flag screener in EVIDENCE-REVIEW.md was — completeness and clinical consistency matter more than RCT grading for a screener).**

A red-flag screener at the foot-pain entry point should HALT the self-management flow and route to a clinician if ANY of the following are present:

- **Suspected stress fracture (metatarsal or navicular specifically):**
  - Focal, pinpoint bony tenderness over a single specific spot (vs. diffuse tenderness across a wider area or along a tendon line) — clinical sources consistently distinguish "one spot on the bone" (fracture) from "spreads along tendon course" (tendinitis) or "across the ball of the foot" (metatarsalgia).
  - Pain present at rest or that wakes the person at night — mechanical/overuse pain typically eases with rest; bone-stress pain characteristically does not.
  - A recent, identifiable spike in training load/volume/impact (the classic bone-stress-injury trigger).
  - Localized swelling directly over the painful spot (vs. diffuse swelling).
  - **Navicular specifically is a named high-risk site**: presents as vague, activity-related dorsal mid-foot pain with focal tenderness over the dorsal proximal navicular; it is notorious for delayed diagnosis (averaging 4 to 9+ months from symptom onset in clinical reviews) because early X-rays are often falsely negative and the presentation is easy to dismiss as "just" foot strain — this is exactly the kind of injury a self-management app must NOT let someone train through. Metatarsal stress fracture (esp. 2nd/3rd metatarsal, or the proximal 5th metatarsal — a separate high-risk "Jones fracture" zone) is the more common but still serious counterpart.
- **Numbness, tingling, or burning radiating into the toes** — the hallmark of Morton's neuroma or other nerve involvement (Section 3d); not something a strengthening/stretching protocol addresses, needs clinical exam.
- **A sudden "pop" with immediate loss of function**, or inability to bear weight — suggests an acute rupture/fracture/severe sprain rather than a gradual-onset overuse condition; this mirrors the existing Ottawa-Ankle-Rules-style screening logic already built into EVIDENCE-REVIEW.md's injury protocol for acute events, and foot pain should route through that same acute-injury pathway rather than the gradual-onset foot-pain flow if it started with a discrete traumatic event.
- **Systemic signs**: fever/chills alongside joint pain (possible septic arthritis — same-day care), or sudden severe redness/heat/swelling especially at the big toe joint overnight (possible gout — a metabolic condition requiring medical management, not a load-management problem; distinguishing feature is the suddenness and inflammatory appearance vs. the gradual mechanical onset of metatarsalgia).
- **No improvement after ~1-2 weeks of appropriate self-management**, or symptoms that are worsening rather than plateauing/improving despite following the protocol — this generic "not responding as expected" flag is standard across all the injury-protocol literature reviewed for this app (mirrors the existing red-flag screener's own catch-all clause) and should apply to foot pain too.
- **Diabetes or known peripheral neuropathy**: not found as a foot-pain-specific citation in this search, but flagged here as a common-sense addition consistent with the app's 40+ audience — reduced sensation changes how reliable "pain" is as a monitoring signal (the entire Section 4 pain-monitoring model assumes the user can feel and accurately report pain), so a user with diagnosed neuropathy/diabetic foot disease should be routed to a clinician rather than self-managed regardless of which zone is tapped. This is a reasoned extension from the program's own logic, not a directly cited source — flagged as such.

**What the app should do:** Build this as a screener at foot-pain logging, structurally identical to the acute-injury red-flag screener already specified in EVIDENCE-REVIEW.md (any flag halts self-rehab and routes to a clinician message) — do not build a second, differently-shaped screening mechanism.

## 6. Time-course + recovery criteria

**Grade: consistent clinical consensus for PF (large observational base + the RCT follow-up data already cited); thin/extrapolated for metatarsalgia (no dedicated long-term cohort data found in this search).**

### 6a. Plantar fasciitis time-course

- With consistent, evidence-based loading (the Rathleff/DiGiovanni protocols above), **meaningful improvement is typically reported within 2-6 weeks**, with **substantial or full resolution most commonly in the 3-6 month range**.
- Left untreated or managed inconsistently, plantar fasciitis is still usually self-limiting but can run **6-18 months**; a minority of cases persist longer and escalate to injection/surgical discussion (the exact population this protocol is trying to keep people OUT of, since DiGiovanni's 2-year follow-up showed the stretching group had fewer progressions to invasive treatment).
- Overall resolution rate with non-surgical management is commonly cited around **~90%** across clinical reviews.
- **Recovery-criteria check (what "recovered enough to resume the plan" looks like):** first-step morning pain resolved or minimal (not "zero forever" — occasional mild stiffness is normal), pain-free during normal daily walking, and able to perform the heel-raise protocol at a meaningful working load without symptom flare — i.e., criteria-based (matching Section 4d's return-to-impact gate), not calendar-based.

### 6b. Metatarsalgia time-course

- Clinical treatment summaries (not dedicated cohort-outcome studies) suggest conservative care typically resolves symptoms in **~4-6 weeks** for straightforward mechanical/overload metatarsalgia, which is notably faster than plantar fasciitis's typical timeline — consistent with metatarsalgia usually being a more mechanically-driven, footwear/load-reversible problem rather than a chronic degenerative tissue-adaptation problem the way PF can become.
- This is a thinner evidence base than the PF timeline above (extrapolated from general clinical practice summaries, not a specific outcomes cohort) — the app should present this range with appropriately softer confidence language than the PF timeline.

### 6c. Relapse prevention (both conditions)

No condition-specific "continue exercises for N weeks after pain-free" RCT was found for either PF or metatarsalgia in this search (a genuine gap, flagged honestly rather than invented). The generalizable principle, consistent with the tendinopathy/overuse literature used elsewhere in this program (Section 4; and the tendon-adaptation logic in EVIDENCE-REVIEW.md Issue #1) is that connective-tissue and load-tolerance adaptations are slow to build and can regress if the loading stimulus is dropped too abruptly. A reasonable, conservative extrapolation — flagged explicitly as extrapolated, not directly cited — is to keep the maintenance dose of the causal exercise (heel raise for PF, calf stretch + short-foot work for metatarsalgia) at a reduced frequency (e.g., dropping from every-other-day to 2x/week) for **at least 4-6 weeks after the person is pain-free and back to full activity**, rather than stopping the exercise the moment pain disappears, mirroring how the main program already treats "feeling ready" as distinct from "tissue actually adapted" (EVIDENCE-REVIEW.md Issue #1's core lesson, reapplied here).

- **Foot-loading strength work specifically (squats, lunges, standing calf work) is treated like running** — modified/monitored via the pain rule rather than a hard pause, since standing/loaded lower-body work also loads the plantar fascia and forefoot, just less repetitively than running.

**The recovery gate that ends the adaptation** (ties together Sections 4d and 6):

1. Pain-free during normal daily walking, AND
2. First-step morning pain resolved or minimal (PF) / no rest-pain or focal bone tenderness (metatarsalgia), AND
3. Able to complete the condition's key strength exercise at a meaningful working load/rep target without symptom flare (20x single-leg heel raise for PF; comparable pain-free intrinsic-foot-work set for metatarsalgia) — this doubles as both the treatment exercise and its own graduation test, which is an elegant, buildable mechanism (no separate "test" needs to be authored).
4. THEN: reintroduce running/impact via the program's EXISTING +10%-session-cap graded-return mechanism (reuse, don't rebuild) rather than resuming at pre-injury volume, with the foot-specific exercises continuing at reduced (not zero) frequency for 4-6 weeks post-recovery as relapse prevention (Section 6c).

Any red flag (Section 5) at any point in this flow should short-circuit straight to "see a clinician," structurally identical to the existing acute/red-flag routing already specified in EVIDENCE-REVIEW.md — this feature should feel like an extension of the program's existing injury-handling architecture, not a parallel system.
